# Facilitator Notes

**AI Without the Sales Pitch. One page. For whoever delivers the classes.**

---

## Suggested pacing (per class, 90 minutes with a break, or trim to 60)

- 0:00 to 0:10: Welcome, show of hands ("who has used one of these tools this month"), promise of the series. Never open with definitions; open with their lives.
- 0:10 to 0:45: Outline points 1 to 6, delivered as talk plus live screen. Show, do not describe: have the tool open and do one real thing while you make the point.
- 0:45 to 0:55: Break. Talk to people. The best material for later classes comes from break-time questions.
- 0:55 to 1:10: Outline points 7 to 12 plus the myth to bust. Deliver the myth last among the content; it is the memory hook.
- 1:10 to 1:25: The practical exercise. Circulate. Pairs, not solo; solo attendees disengage at keyboards.
- 1:25 to 1:30: Debrief, one-line takeaway, homework (install the relevant document from the pack this week), next class teaser.

Class-specific timing notes: Class 3's settings safari needs everyone logged in, so send a "bring your device and your login" reminder 48 hours ahead. Class 5's demo must be rehearsed twice and have a pre-built fallback file, because live AI builds fail on stage with cruel regularity and the failure is only funny the first time.

## Common questions to expect (and the honest one-liner)

- "Which one is best?" → "Best at what? Your existing ecosystem decides more than any benchmark. Doc 6, two-question method."
- "Is it free though? What's the catch?" → "The catch is your data on consumer tiers, and that is Class 3."
- "Will it take my job?" → Document 8, question 1, out loud, no flinching. Audiences respect a straight answer here more than anywhere else.
- "My nephew says the new model is conscious / AGI is next year." → Class 1: a stack of numbers doing pattern-matching; consciousness claims are metaphors doing unpaid work.
- "Can I trust it for my tax return?" → It can explain, draft and organise; a registered agent lodges. Fluent is not verified.
- "What does it cost to set all this up properly?" → Class 6 rollout: a couple of business seats, one page of policy, one week. Under A$150 a month for a ten-person business.
- "Someone in my office is already pasting client stuff into ChatGPT." → That is the amnesty conversation in Class 6; hand them Document 3 tonight, not next month.

## Handling "but I heard that..." objections

The pattern is always the same: a headline, a podcast clip or a brother-in-law, remembered with more confidence than detail. Four moves, in order.

1. **Ask for the source and date.** "Where did you hear that, and roughly when?" Half of objections dissolve here, because the claim predates three model generations or comes from a vendor with something to sell. This is Class 2's field test applied to the objector, gently.
2. **Separate the capability claim from the timeline claim.** "AI can write a whole app" may be true at some level today, for some definition of app, with some supervision; "AI will write all software by next year" is a future-tense marketing sentence. Concede the first, examine the second.
3. **Concede the kernel of truth.** Every persistent myth grew around something real. "AI makes stuff up" is true and is our whole checking discipline. "AI will change work" is true and is Class 5. Agreeing with the kernel buys you the room for the correction.
4. **Offer the test, not the argument.** "Bring the claim to next week and we will run it live on your own data." Nothing converts a sceptic like watching their own document get summarised well, or their own doomsday claim fail the pilot test. Never win an argument with a person in front of a room; win them a demonstration.

Specific recurring ones: "But I heard ChatGPT is being sued for copyright" (true, unresolved, and about the vendors' training data, not about whether you may use the output for your quote emails; watch this space, buy business tiers with indemnity-style terms if it matters to you). "But I heard these tools use enormous amounts of water and power" (true at industry scale, a legitimate reason for the hybrid approach and for not asking a datacentre to do what your laptop can). "But my competitor says AI cut their costs 40%" (Class 2, verbatim: whose measure, verified how; Klarna is the standing case study).

## Room craft

- Demo with the free tiers most attendees actually have, then mention what the paid tier changes. Never demo on Enterprise features nobody in the room will ever touch.
- Keep a printed Doc 4 and Doc 10 on every table. People take paper.
- When someone shares a horror story, thank them and use it. The horror stories are the curriculum; every one of them maps to a checklist in the pack.
- Watch the clock on Class 5; the build exercise always runs long because debugging is public and debugging is slow. Pre-bake the fallback.
- Local government attendees will ask about procurement and records obligations; point them to Class 6's vendor questions plus their own records officer. Do not freelance legal answers.

## Legal line for facilitators

Say this once per class: "Everything in this course is practical guidance, not legal advice. For your specific situation, especially health information or contracts, talk to a lawyer or privacy professional." Then keep teaching. The disclaimer protects you; it does not excuse vagueness.
