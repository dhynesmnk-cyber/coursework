## The policy in one breath

Use AI tools, but use the approved ones with your work account. Never paste passwords, personal details in bulk, or anything confidential. Check everything before it leaves the business. If something goes wrong, tell [Name] the same day, and nobody gets in trouble for reporting.

## 1. Where we stand

We want you using AI tools. They save time and improve drafts, and pretending they don't exist would be like banning email in 1998.

What we can't have is that use happening out of sight, with client data, on free personal accounts, with nobody able to answer for it. This policy brings AI use into the open. It isn't a ban.

Using AI at work is not a disciplinary matter. Hiding a mistake is harder to forgive than making one.

## 2. What "shadow AI" means

Think of using your own ute for a work delivery without telling anyone. You're being helpful, but the business doesn't know, and the insurance doesn't cover it.

**Shadow AI** is the same thing with AI tools: using them for work without the business knowing. Your personal ChatGPT account on your phone. A free chatbot a friend recommended. An AI feature tucked inside some app you installed.

Being resourceful isn't the problem. The problems are that free consumer tools may keep your data, may use it to improve their models unless a setting says otherwise, sit on overseas computers with no contract protecting us, and produce output nobody checked that ends up in front of a customer.

**If you're already using something we haven't approved: tell [Name]. There's no penalty for owning up. We'd much rather make a useful habit official than discover it during an incident.**

## 3. Approved tools

These are the tools we approve and pay for, on business accounts with contract protections for our data:

- [Tool 1, e.g. Microsoft 365 Copilot (sign in with your work account only)]
- [Tool 2, e.g. ChatGPT Business (accounts issued by us; do not use your personal account for work)]
- [Tool 3, e.g. our local/internal assistant (runs in the office; nothing leaves the building)]

Anything not on this list needs a two-minute chat with [Name] first. The list is reviewed quarterly.

## 4. What you may put into an approved AI tool

- General drafting: emails, social posts, letters, meeting summaries, job descriptions.
- Material that's already public, or that has no personal information and no secrets in it.
- Business documents covered by the tool's account tier. (Everything on the approved list is on a tier where our data isn't used to train the vendor's models.)
- Client material, but only if: the tool is on the approved list, you paste the minimum needed, and you follow any client-specific rules. Some contracts restrict AI use; [Name] keeps the list.

## 5. What you must never put into any AI tool

- **Passwords, access keys, logins, payment card numbers.** Never, into anything, ever.
- **Full client lists, or personal information in bulk** (names with contact details, dates of birth, Medicare or tax file numbers, driver's licence numbers), unless [Name] has approved that exact use on a contracted tool.
- **Health or medical information about any person,** unless we're a health provider and [Name] has approved a compliant tool in writing.
- **Anything under a non-disclosure agreement or client confidentiality,** unless that agreement allows it or [Name] says so.
- **Employee complaints, disciplinary matters, payroll details:** approved tools only, the minimum needed, and tell [Name] you're doing it.
- **Financial figures that aren't public yet** (results before they're announced, tender pricing): approved tools only.

**When in doubt, blank it out.** Write "Client A in Toowoomba" instead of the name, and "[amount]" instead of the figure. Put the real details back into the draft afterwards, in your own software.

## 6. Checking the output: the part that's never optional

AI tools are like a confident new starter who never says "I don't know". They write fluently and can be wrong with total confidence. They invent prices, dates, rules, references and quotes. So:

- **You're responsible for anything you send, file or publish,** whether a person or a machine drafted it. "The AI said so" isn't a defence, with us or with a court.
- Check every name, number, date, price, deadline and quoted rule against a source you trust before it leaves the business.
- Anything going to a client, a regulator or the public gets a second pair of eyes: yours, plus the usual approver.
- Never present AI output as checked professional advice (legal, accounting, medical, engineering) unless a qualified person here has actually checked it.

## 7. If you've pasted something sensitive: same-day steps, no drama

1. **Stop and write down what happened.** Which tool, which account, roughly what data, what time.
2. **Tell [Name] the same day.** Reporting on the day is always fine. It's discoveries later on that cause problems.
3. **Delete the chat** in the tool. If the tool has an "exclude from training" or temporary-chat setting, check whether it was on. (It should have been; say so if it wasn't.)
4. **[Name] assesses it** against our privacy obligations: was it personal information, could it cause serious harm, do we have to report it? Usually the answer is "contained, logged, move on". Sometimes there's more to do, and doing it fast is what keeps it small.
5. **We log it** in the incident register: one line, no names, no blame. The log exists to fix patterns, not people.

## 8. Clients, honesty and disclosure

If a client asks whether AI was used on their work, answer honestly and without embarrassment: "We use AI tools for drafting and summarising, on contracted business platforms, and a qualified person checks everything before it reaches you. Here's our policy."

If a client's contract restricts AI use, we follow the contract. We never imply a person did work that a machine did unsupervised, and we never claim checks we didn't do.

## 9. Personal use on work devices

Using AI for your own things, in your own time, on your own account is your business, with two limits: not on systems that hold client data, and never pasting work material into personal accounts. The line is the data, not the device.
