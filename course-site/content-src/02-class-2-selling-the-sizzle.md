# Class 2: Selling the Sizzle
### How to read AI marketing without losing your lunch or your budget

---

## Registration page description (150 words)

A large share of what the public hears about AI is vendor marketing wearing an education costume. This class hands you the tools to see the seams. We work through real, recent, named examples: the big consulting firm that had to refund the Australian government after an AI-drafted report contained invented citations; the fintech that announced AI had replaced 700 customer-service agents and then quietly started rehiring humans; the airlines, fast-food chains and language apps that learned the same lesson the expensive way. From those cases we build a simple field test you can apply to any claim, any demo, any sales call: does this describe what the software does today, on my screen, with my data, or does it ask me to imagine a future that has not arrived? You will leave able to run a vendor meeting like a buyer rather than a believer, with a list of questions that separate capability from brochure.

## Detailed outline

**1. Why AI marketing is unusually thick.**
Three reasons, all commercial. The vendors are in a land-grab and spending billions on datacentres, so the pitch must justify the spend. The technology genuinely improved fast between 2022 and 2024, which gave marketers a real story to inflate. And the product is hard for a layperson to test, because output quality varies from task to task, so a slick demo of the easy 20% can carry a purchase decision for the hard 80%.

**2. The field test.**
Say it once, use it all night: if a claim requires you to imagine a future that hasn't arrived yet, it is marketing. If a claim describes what the software does today, on your screen, with your data, right now, it is probably real. "Our agents will handle your entire customer service operation" is the first kind. "Paste in this 40-page contract and it produces a clause-by-clause summary in about a minute" is the second kind, and you can verify it yourself in the class.

**3. The demo trick and the pilot test.**
Vendor demos are choreographed: pre-selected prompts, forgiving tasks, retries hidden. The counter-move is the pilot test. You supply the data and the task, on your material, in a session you control, with a success measure agreed in writing before the demo starts. If a vendor refuses a pilot on your data, treat the refusal as the answer.

**4. Case study one: Deloitte and the $440,000 report (October 2025).**
Deloitte Australia delivered a report commissioned by the federal Department of Home Affairs, reportedly worth about $440,000 with around $290,000 tied to the affected work, which reviewers found contained AI-generated errors including invented references and fabricated quotations attributed to real academics. Deloitte admitted AI tools were used contrary to its own contractual terms, apologised, and refunded part of the payment. The teaching points: a global professional-services firm with every resource available still shipped confident nonsense; the failure was caught by a human reader who checked citations; and "we use advanced AI" in the brochure did not mean "we verify AI output," which is the part customers actually pay for.

**5. Case study two: Klarna's 700 agents (2024 claim, 2025 reversal).**
In early 2024 the Swedish fintech Klarna announced its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer-service agents and was driving a hiring freeze. By May 2025 its chief executive was publicly admitting the quality push had gone too far and that Klarna would resume hiring human staff to improve service. The teaching points: the first number was a marketing figure with no published method; "handling the volume of X agents" is not the same as "replacing X agents"; and cost cutting advertised as AI transformation often turns out to be cost cutting, which the research firm Forrester has bluntly labelled AI washing.

**6. Case study three: the Air Canada chatbot (decision February 2024).**
A customer asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares, was given wrong information about a refund policy, and was then told by the airline that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions." The British Columbia civil tribunal rejected that argument and ordered the airline to pay the customer's damages. The teaching points: whatever your chatbot tells a customer, your business owns it; and the legal defence "blame the robot" has already failed once, on the record, in a common-law jurisdiction very like ours.

**7. Case study four: the regulators have a word for this now.**
In March 2024 the United States Securities and Exchange Commission fined two investment advisers, Delphia (US$225,000) and Global Predictions (US$175,000), for claiming they used AI in ways they did not; the SEC chair called it "AI washing." Australian regulators, including the ACCC and ASIC, have since signalled the same scrutiny of misleading AI claims under existing consumer and financial-services law. The teaching point: exaggerating AI capability is not a victimless brochure habit; it is becoming an enforcement category, and as a buyer you can ask a vendor what happens to their claims if a regulator tests them.

**8. Case study five: the McDonald's drive-through (June 2024).**
McDonald's ended a trial of IBM's automated order-taker at over 100 US drive-throughs after viral videos of the AI hallucinating orders (bacon on an ice-cream sundae, hundreds of dollars of chicken nuggets). The teaching point: public-facing AI failures are not theoretical, they are filmed, and the reputational cost lands on your brand, not the vendor's.

**9. The vocabulary of the sizzle.**
Learn to flag these words in brochures and translate them on the spot. "Agentic" (the software attempts multi-step tasks with some autonomy; reliability varies wildly by task, ask for your task specifically). "PhD-level" or "expert-level" (a benchmark result on one narrow test, not a job qualification). "10x productivity" (a marketing multiple with no stated baseline). "Enterprise-grade" (a pricing tier, not a quality guarantee). "Powered by AI" (sometimes means one summarisation feature in an otherwise ordinary product). "Seamlessly integrates" (ask which exact system, which exact version, and for a reference customer).

**10. Future-tense tells.**
Concrete grammar-level signals of marketing: heavy use of "will," "soon," "roadmap," "coming later this year," "imagine a world." Ask the vendor to switch every "will" sentence into a present-tense sentence about today's product. Whatever survives that exercise is the product. A live demonstration in 2025 and 2026 has been OpenAI and others publicly describing models approaching "PhD-level" general intelligence on internal roadmaps; the honest buyer response is "noted, and what does this Thursday's version do with my invoices?"

**11. The vendor meeting script.**
Ten questions, in order: (1) Show me my data in your tool, not your sample data. (2) What exactly happens to data I upload, and where is it stored? (3) Do you train on my data, and what tier changes that answer? (4) What is the failure mode when it gets something wrong, and who catches it? (5) Which integration is real today and which is roadmap? (6) Name two customers my size in my industry I can call. (7) What does your contract say about liability for wrong output? (8) What is the total first-year cost including seats, minimums and integration work? (9) What happens to my data and my workflows if I cancel? (10) What did you claim last year that turned out to be wrong? The last question is the character test; the answers to it are reliably revealing.

**12. The internal marketing problem.**
The same discipline applies inward. When a staff member says "AI will save us ten hours a week," run the field test on them: which task, measured how, verified when? When your own board paper says "we are adopting AI," ask what sentence about today's capability would replace it. Class 6 turns this into a procurement and policy template.

## Myth to bust: "If a big, reputable company says it, the capability must be real."

Reputation and accuracy are different products, and the AI era keeps proving it. Deloitte is one of the most established professional-services brands on earth, and in October 2025 it refunded part of a roughly $440,000 report to the Australian Government after reviewers found AI-generated errors including references that did not exist. Klarna's chief executive personally announced AI was doing the work of 700 agents, then spent 2025 explaining that human staff were coming back. Air Canada's lawyers argued in a tribunal that its chatbot was a separate legal entity. None of these organisations is stupid or dishonest in general; all of them were repeating confident AI output or inflating an AI story because it suited a narrative, a share price or a sales cycle. The practical rule for a small business: a claim's credibility comes from the strength of the evidence behind it, never from the size of the logo next to it. Ask for the demo on your data, ask for the reference customer, and check the output yourself. The bigger the brand making the claim, the more comfortable you should feel asking.

## Practical exercise: "Roast this brochure"

**Time: 25 minutes. Needs: printed or projected samples of real AI marketing copy (facilitator prepares four: a chatbot vendor, a "AI receptionist," a copilot bundle, an "agentic automation" platform).**

1. In pairs, attendees take one sample and apply the field test: underline every sentence that describes today's, verifiable behaviour in one colour; circle every sentence that requires imagining the future in another.
2. Rewrite the three most inflated sentences as honest present-tense claims.
3. Draft the three hardest questions from the vendor meeting script that the sample conspicuously avoids answering.
4. Each pair presents one rewrite. The room votes on which vendor would survive the pilot test.
5. Debrief point: notice that the honest rewrites are usually still sellable. Real capability does not need the sizzle, which is exactly why the sizzle is a signal.

## Glossary

- **AI washing:** describing a product, process or layoff as AI-driven when the AI element is minimal, unproven or absent. Now also a regulatory enforcement category in the United States and a stated concern of Australian regulators.
- **Benchmark:** a standard test used to compare models. Useful for vendors; a narrow measure that says little about your specific task.
- **Pilot test:** a short, agreed, measurable trial using your own data and tasks before purchase. The antidote to a choreographed demo.
- **Agentic AI:** software that attempts multi-step tasks (browse, click, email, file) with some autonomy rather than answering one prompt. Reliability is task-dependent; treat every agentic claim as unproven until demonstrated on your workflow.
- **Roadmap:** vendor language for features that do not exist yet. Never buy a roadmap; price the product as it is today.
- **Failure mode:** the characteristic way a tool goes wrong. Every AI tool has one; a mature vendor can describe theirs plainly.
- **Total cost of ownership:** seats plus minimums plus integration plus training plus the staff time spent checking the output. The number that belongs in your budget, not the per-seat headline.
