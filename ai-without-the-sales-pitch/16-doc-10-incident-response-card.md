# Document 10: Incident Response Quick Card

**Part of the "AI Without the Sales Pitch" participant pack. One page. Print it, pin it where the coffee machine is.**
**Business:** [name]. **Policy owner / first call:** [name, mobile]. **Second call:** [name]. **Incident log:** [location].
**Version:** October 2026. Calm, procedural, no drama. Same-day reporting is always the right move.

---

## Scenario 1: An employee realises they pasted sensitive data into an AI tool

*(Client details, financials, health information, passwords, anything under NDA.)*

1. **Stop using that chat. Note the facts:** which tool, which account (personal or work), roughly what data, what time. Two minutes, write it down.
2. **Tell the policy owner the same day.** There is no penalty for same-day reporting. Every hour of delay is the only thing that turns a log entry into an incident.
3. **Delete the conversation** in the tool. On consumer tools also: check Settings for the training/opt-out toggles (Document 5) and note whether they were on. If the tool offers a data-deletion request (most vendors have one in privacy settings or support), the policy owner submits it.
4. **If a password, key or login was pasted: change it immediately**, before anything else on this list. Assume it is compromised. Do not wait for the assessment.
5. **Assess (policy owner, same day):** Was it personal information covered by the Privacy Act? Could it cause serious harm to anyone? Is the tool a consumer tier (worse) or contracted business tier (contained by DPA)? Most cases end here as "logged, contained, coaching."
6. **If the assessment says possible serious harm:** treat it as a potential notifiable data breach. The policy owner (with legal advice if needed) assesses against the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, and if it is eligible, notifies the OAIC and affected individuals. Fast, factual notification is far less damaging than a late discovery.
7. **Log it** (one line: date, tool, category of data, actions taken, outcome) and fix the pattern, not the person: usually the fix is approved tools, the decision drill, or one toolbox talk.

## Scenario 2: A client asks whether AI was used in their deliverable

1. **Answer honestly, specifically and without embarrassment.** Model sentence: "We use AI tools for drafting and summarising, on contracted business platforms where our data isn't used to train their models. A qualified person on our team checked every figure and claim before this reached you. Here is our policy." Attach or offer Document 3.
2. **Check the contract before answering anything beyond that.** If the engagement restricts AI use, the policy owner reviews what was done against what was promised, and we address any gap proactively. Silence and hope is not a strategy; Deloitte's refund is the case study.
3. **Never claim checks that did not happen.** If the deliverable was not human-verified, say what was actually done. A smaller honest claim survives; a large false one ends relationships and can breach consumer law.
4. **Log the question** (date, client, answer given). Repeated questions from clients are market research: they tell you the disclosure paragraph belongs in your standard engagement letter. Ask your lawyer to add one.
5. **Feed it back:** if the question exposed a gap (a tool off the approved list, an unverified draft that went out), run the relevant checklist here and update the policy.

## Scenario 3: A data breach involving an AI vendor is reported in the news

1. **Establish the facts before forming an opinion:** which vendor, which product and tier, what data category, what period, is it confirmed or rumour. Vendor security pages and status pages first, news second.
2. **Check whether we are affected:** does anyone here use that product? Which tier are we on (consumer or contracted business)? Did our usage in the affected window involve personal or sensitive information? The tool register (Document 6 process) makes this a ten-minute job, which is why the register exists.
3. **If we are affected, in scope and on a business tier:** the vendor's DPA obligations kick in. The policy owner contacts the vendor's support/security channel in writing, asks the direct questions (was our data in scope, what was exposed, what remediation, will you notify us formally), and keeps the thread.
4. **Assess our own notification duties:** under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, an eligible breach at our processor can still be our notification problem. If personal information we control was involved and serious harm is likely, get advice promptly and notify the OAIC and affected people. Early, plain-language notice to affected clients beats them reading about it.
5. **Take protective actions regardless:** where feasible, rotate any credentials or keys that touched that vendor; review what we send them going forward; consider pausing sensitive use until their report lands.
6. **If we are not affected:** log the check anyway (one line), and use it as the quarterly reminder to verify the register is current. Next incident, you will be glad it exists.

## Scenario 4: AI-produced output was factually wrong and it went to a customer

*(A wrong price, a fake deadline, an invented policy, a hallucinated citation in something that was sent, published or filed.)*

1. **Contain:** if the wrong output is still live (website, portal, scheduled posts), pull it now. If it was sent, note exactly who received it and when.
2. **Correct to the customer promptly and plainly.** Model: "An error in the [quote/summary/report] we sent on [date]: [the specific error]. The correct position is [fact]. We have corrected the attached version." Own the error as the business; do not blame the software to the customer. Nobody has ever been reassured by "our AI said so." Air Canada tried that in a tribunal and lost.
3. **Assess the damage honestly:** did anyone act on the wrong information (pay, sign, commit, rely)? Money or commitments affected means the policy owner plus, if the amount or exposure is material, your lawyer or insurer, same week not same quarter.
4. **Find the hole in the process, not the villain.** The standard post-mortem questions: was the material supplied to the tool (if not, it invented to fill the gap)? Did the human check step happen (Document 4, Pattern B; Document 7, step 3 and 5)? Was the output type one our policy says needs second eyes? The failure is nearly always a skipped step, and skipped steps are fixable with a checklist, not a lecture.
5. **Fix and log:** add the specific check to the relevant workflow, run a five-minute toolbox talk if the pattern could repeat elsewhere, and log the incident (date, what went out, correction, process fix). Two or three logged incidents of the same shape tell you exactly which workflow needs redesign; that log is one of the most useful documents a small business can keep.

---

**The four scenarios share one rule: speed beats perfection.** Same-day telling, plain-language correcting, and written follow-up resolve almost everything at small scale. What escalates small problems is delay, hiding, and improvising answers under pressure, which is why this card exists and why the first call on it is a person, not a search engine.
