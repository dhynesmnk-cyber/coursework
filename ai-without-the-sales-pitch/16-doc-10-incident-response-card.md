# Document 10: Incident Response Quick Card

**Part of the "AI Without the Sales Pitch" participant pack. One page. Print it, pin it where the coffee machine is.**
**Business:** [name]. **Policy owner / first call:** [name, mobile]. **Second call:** [name]. **Incident log:** [location].
**Version:** October 2026. Calm, procedural, no drama. Same-day reporting is always the right move.

---

## In plain terms

This is the evacuation map for AI mistakes: read it once now, so on the bad day you follow steps instead of panicking. Every scenario has the same spine: stop, tell the policy owner the same day, fix what you can, be honest with anyone affected, and write one line in the log.

---

## Scenario 1: An employee realises they pasted sensitive data into an AI tool

*(Client details, financials, health information, passwords, anything under NDA.)*

1. **Stop using that chat, and write down the facts:** which tool, which account (personal or work), roughly what data, what time. Two minutes.
2. **Tell the policy owner the same day.** There's no penalty for reporting on the day. Delay is the only thing that turns a log entry into an incident.
3. **Delete the conversation** in the tool. On consumer tools, also check the training and opt-out settings (Document 5) and note whether they were on. If the vendor offers a data-deletion request (most have one in privacy settings or through support), the policy owner submits it.
4. **If a password, access key or login was pasted: change it immediately,** before anything else on this list. Assume it's been seen. Don't wait for the assessment.
5. **Assess (policy owner, same day):**
   - Was it personal information covered by the Privacy Act?
   - Could it cause serious harm to anyone?
   - Was the tool a free consumer account (worse) or a contracted business account (contained by its data processing agreement)?

   Most cases end here: "logged, contained, a quick word about the decision drill".
6. **If serious harm is possible:** treat it as a potential notifiable data breach. The policy owner, with legal advice if needed, checks it against the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme. If it qualifies, notify the privacy regulator (the OAIC) and the affected people. Fast, factual notice does far less damage than a late discovery.
7. **Log it** in one line (date, tool, type of data, what was done, outcome) and fix the pattern, not the person. The fix is usually approved tools, the decision drill, or one toolbox talk.

## Scenario 2: A client asks whether AI was used in their deliverable

1. **Answer honestly, specifically and without embarrassment.** Model sentence: "We use AI tools for drafting and summarising, on contracted business platforms where our data isn't used to train their models. A qualified person on our team checked every figure and claim before this reached you. Here is our policy." Attach or offer Document 3.
2. **Check the contract before saying anything more.** If the engagement restricts AI use, the policy owner compares what was done with what was promised, and we raise any gap ourselves. Staying quiet and hoping isn't a strategy; Deloitte's refund is the case study.
3. **Never claim checks that didn't happen.** If nobody checked the work, say what was actually done. A smaller honest claim survives. A large false one ends relationships and can breach consumer law.
4. **Log the question** (date, client, answer given). Repeated questions from clients are market research: they tell you a disclosure paragraph belongs in your standard engagement letter. Ask your lawyer to add one.
5. **Feed it back.** If the question exposed a gap (a tool that isn't on the approved list, an unchecked draft that went out), run the relevant checklist here and update the policy.

## Scenario 3: A data breach involving an AI vendor is reported in the news

1. **Get the facts before forming an opinion:** which vendor, which product and tier, what type of data, what time period, and is it confirmed or rumour? Check the vendor's security and status pages first, the news second.
2. **Check whether we're affected:** does anyone here use that product? Which tier are we on (consumer or contracted business)? Did our use during the affected period involve personal or sensitive information? The tool register (Class 6) makes this a ten-minute job, which is exactly why the register exists.
3. **If we're affected and on a business tier:** the vendor's obligations under its data processing agreement kick in. The policy owner writes to the vendor's support or security team and asks directly: was our data involved, what was exposed, what are you doing about it, and will you notify us formally? Keep the email thread.
4. **Work out our own duty to notify:** under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, a breach at our vendor can still be our responsibility to report. If personal information we control was involved and serious harm is likely, get advice promptly, and notify the OAIC and the people affected. Early, plain-language notice to affected clients beats them reading about it in the news.
5. **Take protective steps regardless:** where you can, change any passwords or access keys that were used with that vendor; review what we send them from now on; consider pausing sensitive use until their report is out.
6. **If we're not affected:** log the check anyway (one line), and use it as a reminder to confirm the register is up to date. Next time, you'll be glad it exists.

## Scenario 4: AI-produced output was wrong and it went to a customer

*(A wrong price, a fake deadline, an invented policy, a made-up reference in something that was sent, published or filed.)*

1. **Contain it.** If the wrong output is still live (website, portal, scheduled posts), take it down now. If it was sent, note exactly who received it and when.
2. **Correct it with the customer, promptly and plainly.** Model: "An error in the [quote/summary/report] we sent on [date]: [the specific error]. The correct position is [fact]. We have corrected the attached version." Own the error as the business; don't blame the software to the customer. Nobody has ever been reassured by "our AI said so". Air Canada tried that argument before a tribunal and lost.
3. **Assess the damage honestly.** Did anyone act on the wrong information: pay, sign, commit, rely on it? If money or commitments are involved, bring in the policy owner, and if the amount or risk is significant, your lawyer or insurer. The same week, not the same quarter.
4. **Find the hole in the process, not a villain.** Ask:
   - Was the real material given to the tool? If not, it filled the gap by inventing, the bluffing student from Class 1.
   - Did the human check happen? (Document 4, Pattern B; Document 7, Steps 3 and 5.)
   - Was this a type of output our policy says needs a second pair of eyes?

   The cause is nearly always a skipped step, and skipped steps are fixed with a checklist, not a lecture.
5. **Fix it and log it.** Add the specific check to the workflow, run a five-minute toolbox talk if it could happen elsewhere, and log the incident (date, what went out, the correction, the process fix). Two or three logged incidents of the same shape tell you exactly which workflow needs redesigning. That log is one of the most useful documents a small business can keep.

---

**All four scenarios share one rule: speed beats perfection.** Telling someone the same day, correcting in plain language and following up in writing sorts out almost everything at small scale. What makes small problems big is delay, hiding, and improvising answers under pressure. That's why this card exists, and why the first call on it is a person, not a search engine.
