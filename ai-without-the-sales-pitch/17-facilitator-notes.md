# Facilitator Notes

**AI Without the Sales Pitch. For whoever delivers the classes.**

---

## How this course teaches: the Feynman Technique

The physicist Richard Feynman's test was simple: if you can't explain something simply, you don't understand it yet. Every class is built on that test, and your delivery should be too.

**The four moves, in every class:**

1. **Picture first, word second.** Open each idea with an everyday picture the room already knows, then name the technical term and translate it. Never the other way round. "The model has a desk, and the size of the desk is called the context window" lands. "The context window is the maximum token count" doesn't.
2. **One picture per idea, used consistently.** The learners read the same pictures in their chapters, so use the same ones out loud. Switching analogies mid-course costs people the thread.
3. **Say where the picture breaks.** Analogies mislead if you stretch them. Each chapter has a "where the picture breaks" note; mention it. It builds trust, and it stops the sharp person in the second row from derailing you.
4. **Make them explain it back.** Understanding shows up when people try to explain. The "Explain it back" box at the end of each chapter is the debrief: pairs, three sentences each, no jargon allowed.

**The picture kit** (keep these consistent across all six classes):

| Idea | The picture |
|---|---|
| What a model is | Phone autocomplete at enormous scale; a sound desk with billions of locked sliders |
| Hallucination | The student bluffing an exam essay: knows what a good answer sounds like, not what's true |
| Tokens | The meter running: like kilowatt-hours on a power bill |

## Suggested pacing (per class, 90 minutes with a break, or trim to 60)

- **0:00 to 0:10:** welcome, a show of hands ("who has used one of these tools this month?"), and the promise of the series. Never open with definitions; open with their lives. Read "The short version" aloud as the promise of the night.
- **0:10 to 0:45:** the first half of the chapter, as talk plus a live screen. Show, don't describe: have the tool open and do one real thing while you make each point.
- **0:45 to 0:55:** break. Talk to people. The best material for later classes comes from break-time questions.
- **0:55 to 1:10:** the second half of the chapter, finishing with "Myth, meet reality". Deliver the myth last; it's the memory hook.
- **1:10 to 1:25:** the practical exercise. Circulate. Work in pairs, not solo; people on their own disengage at keyboards.
- **1:25 to 1:30:** "Explain it back" in pairs: each person explains the night's main idea to their partner in three sentences, no jargon, and the partner points out any word that needed translating. Then a one-line takeaway, homework (install the relevant document from the pack this week), and a teaser for next class.

**Class-specific timing notes:** Class 3's settings safari needs everyone logged in, so send a "bring your device and your login" reminder 48 hours ahead. Class 5's demo must be rehearsed twice with a pre-built fallback file, because live AI builds fail on stage with cruel regularity, and the failure is only funny the first time.

## Common questions to expect (and the honest one-liner)

- **"Which one is best?"** "Best at what? The software you already use decides more than any test score. Document 6, the two-question method."
- **"Is it free though? What's the catch?"** "The catch is your data on consumer accounts. That's Class 3."
- **"Will it take my job?"** Document 8, question 1, out loud, no flinching. Audiences respect a straight answer here more than anywhere else.
- **"My nephew says the new model is conscious / AGI is next year."** Class 1: a stack of numbers doing pattern-matching, the bluffing student. "Conscious" is a metaphor doing unpaid work.
- **"Can I trust it for my tax return?"** It can explain, draft and organise; a registered agent lodges. Fluent isn't the same as checked.
- **"What does it cost to set all this up properly?"** The Class 6 rollout: a couple of business seats, one page of policy, one week. Under A$150 a month for a ten-person business.
- **"Someone in my office is already pasting client stuff into ChatGPT."** That's the amnesty conversation in Class 6. Hand them Document 3 tonight, not next month.

## Handling "but I heard that..." objections

The pattern is always the same: a headline, a podcast clip or a brother-in-law, remembered with more confidence than detail. Four moves, in order:

1. **Ask for the source and date.** "Where did you hear that, and roughly when?" Half of objections dissolve here, because the claim is three model generations old or came from someone with something to sell. This is Class 2's field test, applied gently to the objector.
2. **Separate what it can do today from what someone predicts.** "AI can write a whole app" may be true today, for some kinds of app, with supervision. "AI will write all software by next year" is a future-tense marketing sentence. Concede the first; examine the second.
3. **Agree with the grain of truth.** Every lasting myth grew around something real. "AI makes stuff up" is true, and it's the reason for our whole checking habit. "AI will change work" is true, and it's Class 5. Agreeing with the grain of truth earns you room for the correction.
4. **Offer a test, not an argument.** "Bring the claim next week and we'll run it live on your own material." Nothing converts a sceptic like watching their own document get summarised well, or their own doomsday claim fail a pilot. Never win an argument with a person in front of a room; win them a demonstration.

**Specific recurring ones:**
- **"I heard ChatGPT is being sued for copyright."** True and unresolved, and it's about how the vendors trained their models, not about whether you may use the output for your quote emails. Watch this space, and buy business tiers with protective terms if it matters to you.
- **"I heard these tools use enormous amounts of water and power."** True at industry scale, and a legitimate reason for the hybrid approach and for not asking a datacentre to do what your laptop can.
- **"My competitor says AI cut their costs 40%."** Class 2, word for word: whose measure, checked how? Klarna is the standing case study.

## Room craft

- Demo with the free tiers most attendees actually have, then mention what the paid tier changes. Never demo enterprise features nobody in the room will ever touch.
- Keep printed copies of Documents 4 and 10 on every table. People take paper.
- When someone shares a horror story, thank them and use it. The horror stories are the curriculum; every one of them maps to a checklist in the pack.
- If you catch yourself using a technical word before its picture, stop and back up. The room will forgive the restart; it won't forgive being lost.
- Watch the clock in Class 5. The build exercise always runs long, because debugging is public and debugging is slow. Have the fallback ready.
- Local government attendees will ask about procurement and record-keeping obligations. Point them to Class 6's vendor questions plus their own records officer. Don't improvise legal answers.

## Legal line for facilitators

Say this once per class: "Everything in this course is practical guidance, not legal advice. For your specific situation, especially health information or contracts, talk to a lawyer or privacy professional." Then keep teaching. The disclaimer protects you; it doesn't excuse vagueness.
