# Document 3: AI Use Policy (Plain English)

**[Business Name]. For every employee, contractor and volunteer.**
**Owner of this policy: [Name, role]. Questions and reports go to: [email / phone].**
**Version [X], [date]. Reviewed quarterly.**

*Hand this to every person who works here. Read it once properly, keep it somewhere findable. It is two pages because anything longer does not get read.*

---

## 1. Where we stand

We want you using AI tools. They save time, they improve drafts, and pretending they do not exist would be like banning email in 1998. What we cannot do is have that usage happening invisibly, with client data, on free personal accounts, with nobody able to answer for it. This policy exists to bring AI use into the open, not to ban it. Using AI at work is not a disciplinary matter. Hiding a mistake is harder to forgive than making one.

## 2. What "shadow AI" means

Shadow AI is using AI tools for work without the business knowing: your personal ChatGPT account on your phone, a free chatbot a friend recommended, an AI feature buried in some app you installed. The risk is not that you are doing something wrong by being resourceful. The risks are that free consumer tools may keep your data, may use it to improve their models unless settings say otherwise, sit on overseas servers with no contract protecting us, and produce output nobody checked that ends up in front of a customer.

**If you are already using something we have not approved: tell [Name]. There is no penalty for declaring it. We would much rather formalise a useful habit than discover it in an incident.**

## 3. Approved tools

These are the tools we approve and pay for, on business accounts with contractual data protections:

- [Tool 1, e.g. Microsoft 365 Copilot (sign in with your work account only)]
- [Tool 2, e.g. ChatGPT Business (accounts issued by us; do not use your personal account for work)]
- [Tool 3, e.g. our local/internal assistant (runs in the office; nothing leaves the building)]

Anything not on this list needs a two-minute conversation with [Name] first. The list is reviewed quarterly.

## 4. What you may put into an approved AI tool

- General drafting: emails, social posts, letters, meeting summaries, job descriptions.
- Material that is already public, or that contains no personal information and no secrets.
- Business documents that the tool's account tier covers (everything on the approved list is on a tier where our data is not used to train the vendor's models).
- Client material, only if: the tool is on the approved list, you paste the minimum needed, and you follow any client-specific restriction (some contracts restrict AI use; [Name] keeps the list).

## 5. What you must never put into any AI tool

- **Passwords, keys, logins, payment card numbers.** Never, into anything, ever.
- **Full client lists, or personal information in bulk** (names with contact details, dates of birth, Medicare or tax file numbers, driver's licence numbers) unless [Name] has approved that exact use on a contracted tool.
- **Health or medical information about any person**, unless we are a health provider and [Name] has approved a compliant tool in writing.
- **Anything under a non-disclosure agreement or client confidentiality** unless the specific NDA permits it or [Name] says so.
- **Employee grievances, disciplinary matters, payroll detail:** on approved tools only, minimised, and tell [Name] you are doing it.
- **Financials that are not yet public** (pre-announcement results, tender pricing): on approved tools only.

When in doubt, redact: "Client A in Toowoomba" instead of the name; "[amount]" instead of the figure. You can swap the real details back into the draft afterwards, in your own software.

## 6. Checking the output: the non-negotiable part

AI tools write fluently and are wrong with total confidence. They invent prices, dates, rules, references and quotes. So:

- **You remain responsible for anything you send, file or publish**, whether a human or a machine drafted it. "The AI said so" is not a defence, with us or with a court.
- Check every name, number, date, price, deadline and quoted rule against a source you trust before it leaves this business.
- Anything going to a client, a regulator, or the public gets a second pair of eyes: yours plus the usual approver.
- Never present AI output as verified professional advice (legal, accounting, medical, engineering) unless a qualified person here has actually verified it.

## 7. If you have already pasted something sensitive: same-day procedure, no drama

1. **Stop and note what happened.** Which tool, which account, roughly what data, what time.
2. **Tell [Name] the same day.** Same-day reporting is always fine. Discoveries are what cause problems.
3. **Delete the chat/conversation** in the tool, and if the tool has an "exclude from training" or temporary-chat setting, check it was on (it should have been; report if not).
4. **[Name] assesses it** against our privacy obligations: was it personal information? Could it cause serious harm? Is it notifiable? Usually the answer is "contained, logged, move on." Sometimes there is more to do, and doing it fast is what makes it small.
5. **We log it** in the incident register, one line, no names attached to blame. The log exists to fix patterns, not people.

## 8. Clients, honesty, and disclosure

If a client asks whether AI was used on their work, the answer is honest and unembarrassed: "We use AI tools for drafting and summarising, on contracted business platforms, and a qualified person checks everything before it reaches you. Here is our policy." If a client's contract restricts AI use, we follow the contract. We never imply a human did work that a machine did unsupervised, and we never claim checks we did not perform.

## 9. Personal use on work devices

Using AI tools for your own stuff on your own time and your own account is your business, with two limits: not on systems holding client data, and never pasting work material into personal accounts. The line is the data, not the device.
