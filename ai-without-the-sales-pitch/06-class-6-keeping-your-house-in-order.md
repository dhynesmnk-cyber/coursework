# Class 6: Keeping Your House in Order
### Shadow AI, data security basics, and the paperwork that protects a small business

**Time:** about 20 minutes of reading, plus a 30-minute rollout draft.
**By the end of this class you can:** explain shadow AI without villainising anyone, run a one-week governance rollout for a business up to about fifty people, answer "did you use AI on my file?" honestly and calmly, and name the single role that makes all of it stick.

## The short version

Your staff are already using AI, whether or not you've said they can. Banning it just hides it. Instead, approve a few safe tools, write one page of rules, make it easy and blame-free to own up to mistakes, and put one named person in charge. That takes a week.

## Start with the uncomfortable truth

Your staff are already using AI. Every workplace survey since 2023 has found the same thing: use runs well ahead of policy, everywhere, including in businesses that think they've banned it.

The only open question is whether you know where it's happening, with what data, and what happens when something goes wrong. This class closes that gap in one week, without blame, because blame is what keeps the next mistake hidden.

## Shadow AI, defined plainly

Picture a staff member who uses their own ute for a work delivery because the van was booked. They're being helpful. But the business doesn't know, the insurance doesn't cover it, and if something goes wrong, nobody finds out until it's a problem.

**Shadow AI** is the same thing with AI tools: staff using them for work without the business knowing or approving. Pasting the client list into a free chatbot to tidy it up. Drafting a tender response on a personal account at the kitchen table. Summarising a roster that includes everyone's home addresses in a phone app.

It's called "shadow" because the business can't see it, not because the staff are villains. Usually they're being enterprising with the tools they have, which is a trait you otherwise want.

## Why it's a genuine risk, in three lines

- **Data risk.** Free consumer tools may keep what you paste, may use it to improve their models unless a switch says otherwise, sit on overseas computers, and come with no contract you can point to.
- **Accuracy risk.** Unchecked output reaches customers. The Deloitte and Air Canada cases in Class 2 showed what that looks like at scale.
- **Legal risk.** Under the Privacy Act, you stay responsible for the personal information your business collects, including when an employee sends it to a US chatbot from their phone. That's the APP 8 and APP 11 point from Class 3.

## Why bans fail, and what works instead

Banning AI in 2026 is like banning the photocopier in 1986. You can't enforce it, you can't see whether it's working, and it guarantees that whatever use does happen is hidden, which is exactly the risk you were trying to reduce.

What works is an **amnesty plus approved tools**. Think of a library fine amnesty: bring the overdue books back, no fine, and the library finally learns what's been missing. Here's the business version:

1. Approve two or three tools, on business tiers.
2. Publish a one-page policy saying what may and may not be pasted where.
3. Set up a no-blame way to report "oops" moments.
4. Ask teams what they're already using, with no consequences for honesty. Turn the useful habits into official ones, and move the risky ones to the right tool.

Document 3 is exactly this policy, written to be handed out rather than filed.

## The four habits that prevent almost every incident

Every commercial kitchen runs on a few food-safety habits: wash your hands, separate raw and cooked, check the temperature, and report a cut straight away. Nobody finds them exciting, and they prevent almost every disaster. AI at work has its own four:

1. **Work data on work accounts.** Only use business-tier accounts or local tools for work data, because that's where the contract protections are. Check which account you're signed into before you paste, every time. The icons look alike.
2. **The decision drill before pasting.** Personal information, secrets, or anything under a non-disclosure agreement? If yes, change tool or remove the sensitive parts. It's from Class 3, repeated here because it does most of the work.
3. **A person checks everything that leaves the building.** Names, numbers, dates, quotes, references. Checking is usually faster than doing the task from scratch, which is why the honest claim is "faster with a review", not "automatic".
4. **Report the same day.** If something sensitive went where it shouldn't, tell the policy owner that day, with no drama. It's like reporting a scratch on the work car: reported on the day it's a note in a logbook, discovered a month later it's a problem. Delay is the only thing that turns an incident into a crisis.

## The one-page policy, walked through

Every workplace has a "how we do things here" sheet on the staffroom wall. Document 3 is that sheet for AI. Read it once, properly.

It covers where the business stands ("we want you using these tools well, not secretly"), the approved tools, what may be pasted, what must never be pasted, the checking rule, the same-day "oops" procedure, and being honest with clients.

Your job this week is to make it yours. Replace the bracketed business name and contact person, cross out anything that doesn't apply, and add one rule specific to your industry. A medical centre adds "no patient identifiers, ever". A law firm adds "nothing covered by client privilege without the principal's approval".

One page, one read, one signature line. Anything longer doesn't get read, which is why it's one page.

## The incident card, walked through

Think of the evacuation map by the lift. You read it once, calmly, so that on the bad day you don't have to think.

Document 10 is that map for AI incidents. It covers the four that actually happen, each as a short, calm checklist:

- An employee realises they pasted sensitive data into a consumer tool.
- A client asks whether AI was used on their work.
- A vendor breach hits the news.
- Wrong AI output went to a customer.

Read all four once before you need them, then pin the card where people can see it. Rehearse one out loud. Really, out loud. The scenario most worth practising is the client question, and the honest answer, prepared in advance, is: "Yes, where it was used, here's what a person checked, and here's our policy." Delivered calmly, that answer builds trust. Improvised under pressure, the same facts sound like a confession.

## Keeping track of your tools

You won't run big-company procurement, and you don't need to. Three habits carry most of the value:

- **Keep a tool register.** One line per AI tool in use: name, tier, who uses it, and what data touches it. It's an asset register, like the list of who has which laptop.
- **Check before you buy.** Run the Class 2 vendor questions and get the business-tier terms and data processing agreement in writing.
- **Review it every quarter,** in the same calendar slot as the pricing and privacy-settings review from Documents 5 and 6, because tiers, prices and policies all change.

## Disclosure: when to say AI was involved

There's no blanket Australian law requiring ordinary business documents to be labelled as AI-assisted. Specific duties are tightening anyway, so work from principles:

- **Disclose when asked,** honestly and without theatre.
- **Disclose up front where the work is advice:** legal, accounting, medical, engineering, or anything safety-related.
- **Follow any contract that restricts AI use.** Breaching one is a commercial and legal problem regardless of AI law, as Deloitte discovered.
- **Never imply a person checked work that nobody checked.** Misleading-conduct rules apply. More importantly, being caught ends relationships that honest disclosure would have strengthened.

## One person in charge

Every workplace has a named fire warden. Not because they'll fight the fire alone, but because on the day, everyone knows who's checking the floor.

Name one person to own the policy, the tool register, the quarterly review and the incident log. In a ten-person business, that's often the owner or the office manager. Give them the document pack and half an hour a month.

Ownership is the difference between paperwork and practice. Every failed rollout in every industry comes down to nobody owning it, and every successful one comes down to somebody doing exactly this.

## The regulatory horizon, briefly

Australian privacy reform is arriving in stages. The direction is clear even where the dates aren't.

- The first round of Privacy Act changes is in force, with stronger powers for the regulator, higher penalties, and since June 2025, the right to sue for serious invasions of privacy.
- The duty to explain automated decision-making in privacy policies arrives on 10 December 2026.
- Further rounds, including the future of the small-business exemption, are still in the policy pipeline.

The sensible stance is to run your business as if the exemption won't save you, because it's shrinking and because good practice here is cheap: one page, one owner, one week.

## The one-week rollout plan

- **Day one:** pick your named owner and your two or three approved tools.
- **Day two:** circulate Document 3 with a covering note that says, in your own words, "we want you using these well, not secretly".
- **Day three:** collect the amnesty answers. What's already in use, no consequences. Turn the useful parts into official practice.
- **Day four:** move approved tools onto business tiers, or set up the local option. Apply the opt-out settings from Document 5 to anything still on consumer accounts in the meantime.
- **Day five:** pin Document 10 where people can see it, and run a ten-minute **toolbox talk** (a short team briefing, usually at the start of a shift) using Document 8, the plain-English FAQ.

That's a complete AI governance rollout for a business of up to about fifty people, done in a week, for the cost of a subscription or two. No version of this needs a consultant, a project board or a quarter.

## Myth, meet reality

**"We're too small to be a target, so AI data risks don't apply to us."** A burglar walking down the street tries every door handle. They don't check how many staff work inside first.

Attackers and data brokers pick victims by weakness, not size. A small business with staff pasting client data into free chatbots, no written policy, no tool register and no incident plan is weak in exactly the ways that matter. Human error and stolen passwords cause most real incidents, far more than movie-style hacking, and a chatbot account on a personal email with a reused password sits squarely in that category.

"Too small" also fails as a legal defence. The Privacy Act's core duties already apply to many small organisations regardless of turnover, the right to sue for serious invasions of privacy has no turnover test, and your clients increasingly pass their obligations down to you through contracts. In a tender, "we're too small for that" loses to a competitor holding this course's one-page policy.

The right conclusion isn't fear. It's that the controls a small business needs are proportionately small: one page, one owner, one week, and this class hands you all three.

## Words used in this class

- **Shadow AI:** AI tools used for work without the business knowing or approving. Invisible use, not malicious use. The personal ute on a work delivery.
- **AI use policy:** the one page telling staff which tools are approved, what may be pasted, what must be checked, and who to tell. Document 3.
- **Tool register:** one line per tool: name, tier, who uses it, what data touches it, when it was last reviewed.
- **Amnesty:** a no-consequences window for staff to declare the AI tools they already use, so the use can be made official or corrected. The single most effective fix for shadow AI.
- **Incident:** sensitive data pasted where it shouldn't have been, wrong output reaching a customer, or a vendor breach affecting you. Document 10 turns each into a checklist.
- **Owner:** the one named person accountable for the policy, register, reviews and incident log. The fire warden. Without this role, everything else is decoration.
- **Disclosure:** telling a client, customer or reader that AI was involved in producing work. Required by some contracts, and a trust-builder almost everywhere else.
- **Flow-down obligations:** requirements your clients or funders write into contracts that become your requirements. They increasingly include AI rules.
- **OAIC:** the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, the privacy regulator. Its generative AI guidance is the plainest official statement of what "reasonable steps" look like.
- **Toolbox talk:** a short team briefing, five to fifteen minutes, usually at the start of a shift. The right format for rolling out Documents 3, 8 and 10.
