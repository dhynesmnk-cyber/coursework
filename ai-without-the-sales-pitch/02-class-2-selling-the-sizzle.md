# Class 2: Selling the Sizzle
### How to read AI marketing without losing your lunch or your budget

**Time:** about 25 minutes of reading, plus a 15-minute exercise.
**By the end of this class you can:** apply a two-line field test to any AI claim, recognise five marketing tells by name, run a vendor demo like a buyer, and use ten questions that separate capability from brochure.

## The field test, in two lines

If a claim requires you to imagine a future that has not arrived yet, it is marketing. If a claim describes what the software does today, on your screen, with your data, right now, it is probably real.

That is the whole tool. "Our agents will handle your entire customer service operation" is the first kind. "Paste in this 40-page contract and it produces a clause-by-clause summary in about a minute" is the second kind, and you can verify it yourself before lunch. Everything else in this class is practice at applying those two lines under pressure, in a meeting, with a confident person on the other side of the table.

## Why AI marketing is unusually thick

Three reasons, all commercial, none mysterious. The vendors are in a land grab and have spent billions on datacentres, so the pitch must justify the spend. The technology genuinely improved fast between 2022 and 2024, which handed marketers a real story to inflate. And output quality varies from task to task, so a choreographed demo of the easy 20 percent can carry a purchase decision for the hard 80 percent. Knowing why the sizzle exists makes it easier to hear past it without becoming cynical about the genuinely useful product underneath.

## Demos are theatre. Pilots are evidence.

Vendor demos are choreographed: pre-selected prompts, forgiving tasks, retries hidden. The counter-move is a pilot test. You supply the data and the task, on your material, in a session you control, with a success measure agreed in writing before anyone opens a laptop. If a vendor refuses a pilot on your data, treat the refusal as the answer. It is the single most informative sentence a salesperson can say.

## Five case studies, and what each one teaches

**Deloitte and the refund (October 2025).** Deloitte Australia delivered a report to the federal Department of Home Affairs, reportedly worth about $440,000, which reviewers found contained AI-generated errors including invented references and fabricated quotations attributed to real academics. Deloitte admitted AI tools were used contrary to its own contractual terms and refunded part of the payment. The lesson is not "consultants are careless". It is that a global firm with every resource available still shipped confident nonsense, and the error was caught by a human reader who checked the citations. Checking is the product you are actually paying for.

**Klarna's 700 agents (claimed 2024, walked back 2025).** The Swedish fintech announced its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer-service agents. By May 2025 its chief executive was publicly admitting the quality push had gone too far and that human staff were coming back. The lesson: "handles the volume of 700 agents" is a marketing figure with no published method, and cost cutting advertised as AI transformation often turns out to be plain cost cutting. Analysts now have a term for dressing ordinary cuts in AI language: AI washing.

**Air Canada's chatbot (tribunal decision, February 2024).** A customer asked the airline's website chatbot about bereavement fares, received wrong information, and the airline then argued the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected that argument and ordered the airline to pay. The lesson belongs on your office wall: whatever your chatbot tells a customer, your business owns it.

**The regulators have a word for this (March 2024, United States).** The Securities and Exchange Commission fined two investment advisers, Delphia and Global Predictions, US$225,000 and US$175,000 for claiming AI capabilities they did not have. Australian regulators have signalled the same scrutiny under existing consumer law. The lesson: exaggerated AI claims are becoming an enforcement category, which means you can ask a vendor what happens to their claims if a regulator tests them, and watch the face.

**McDonald's drive-through (trial ended June 2024).** The chain ended a trial of automated order-taking after viral videos of the system adding bacon to ice cream and hundreds of dollars of nuggets to ordinary orders. The lesson: public-facing AI failures are filmed, and the reputational cost lands on your brand, not the vendor's.

## The sizzle vocabulary, translated on the spot

- **Agentic:** the software attempts multi-step tasks with some autonomy. Reliability varies wildly by task, so ask to see your task.
- **PhD-level or expert-level:** a result on one narrow benchmark (a standard test used to compare models), not a job qualification.
- **10x productivity:** a multiple with no stated baseline. Ask "ten times what, measured how, by whom?"
- **Enterprise-grade:** a pricing tier, not a quality guarantee.
- **Powered by AI:** sometimes means one summarisation feature bolted onto an ordinary product. Ask which feature, exactly.
- **Seamlessly integrates:** ask which system, which version, and for a reference customer you may call.

## Future-tense tells

Watch the grammar. Heavy use of "will", "soon", "on our roadmap" (roadmap meaning features that do not exist yet) and "imagine a world" is the sound of a brochure. Ask the vendor to repeat every "will" sentence as a present-tense sentence about today's product. Whatever survives that exercise is the product you can buy. The rest is a hope with a price tag.

## Take these ten questions to your next demo

1. Show me my data in your tool, not your sample data.
2. What exactly happens to data I upload, and where is it stored?
3. Do you train on my data, and which pricing tier changes that answer?
4. What is the failure mode (the characteristic way this tool goes wrong) and who catches it?
5. Which integration is real today and which is roadmap?
6. Name two customers my size in my industry I can call.
7. What does your contract say about liability for wrong output?
8. What is the total first-year cost, including seats, minimums and integration work?
9. What happens to my data and my workflows if I cancel?
10. What did you claim last year that turned out to be wrong?

Question ten is the character test. The answers to it are reliably revealing, and a vendor who answers it well has earned the rest of the meeting.

## Point the test inward

The same discipline applies to your own building. When a staff member says AI will save ten hours a week, ask which task, measured how, verified when. When your board paper says "we are adopting AI", replace the sentence with one claim about today's capability and see if it still stands. Class 6 turns this habit into paperwork your team can actually use.

## Myth, meet reality

**"If a big, reputable company says it, the capability must be real."** Reputation and accuracy are different products. Deloitte is one of the most established professional-services brands on earth and still refunded part of a government report over invented citations. Klarna's chief executive personally announced the 700-agent figure and personally walked it back. Air Canada's lawyers argued a chatbot was a separate legal entity, on the record, and lost. None of these organisations is stupid. All of them repeated confident AI output or an inflated AI story because it suited a narrative, a share price or a sales cycle. A claim's credibility comes from the evidence behind it, never from the size of the logo beside it.

## Your exercise: roast one brochure

1. Find one piece of real AI marketing: a vendor email, a product page, a flyer, a sponsored post.
2. Underline every sentence that describes verifiable behaviour today. Circle every sentence that requires imagining the future.
3. Rewrite your three most circled sentences as honest present-tense claims.
4. Write the three hardest questions from the ten above that this material conspicuously avoids answering.
5. Decide, in one sentence: would this vendor survive a pilot test on your data? Notice that your honest rewrites are usually still sellable. Real capability does not need the sizzle, which is exactly why the sizzle is a signal.

## Words used in this class

- **AI washing:** describing a product, process or layoff as AI-driven when the AI element is minimal, unproven or absent. Now also a regulatory enforcement category.
- **Benchmark:** a standard test used to compare models. Useful for vendors, narrow for buyers.
- **Pilot test:** a short, agreed, measurable trial on your own data and tasks before purchase.
- **Agentic AI:** software that attempts multi-step tasks with some autonomy instead of answering one prompt. Treat every agentic claim as unproven until demonstrated on your workflow.
- **Roadmap:** vendor language for features that do not exist yet. Never pay for a roadmap.
- **Failure mode:** the characteristic way a tool goes wrong. A mature vendor can describe theirs plainly.
- **Total cost of ownership:** seats plus minimums plus integration plus training plus the staff time spent checking output. The number that belongs in your budget.
