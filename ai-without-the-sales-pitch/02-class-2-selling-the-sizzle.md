# Class 2: Selling the Sizzle
### How to read AI marketing without losing your lunch or your budget

**Time:** about 25 minutes of reading, plus a 15-minute exercise.
**By the end of this class you can:** apply a two-line field test to any AI claim, recognise five marketing tells by name, run a vendor demo like a buyer, and use ten questions that separate capability from brochure.

## The short version

AI marketing sells you a future. Your job is to buy only the present. If a claim describes what the software does today, with your data, on your screen, you can test it. If it asks you to imagine what it will do, it's a brochure.

## You already know how to do this

You've read real estate listings. You know "cosy" means small, "character-filled" means old, and "close to transport" means a train line at the back fence. Nobody taught you that in a class. You learned it by seeing enough listings and enough houses.

AI marketing has its own version of "cosy", and this class is the translation guide. The skill underneath is the same one you already use on real estate: notice when a word is doing a job other than describing the thing.

## The field test, in two lines

If a claim needs you to imagine a future that hasn't arrived yet, it's marketing. If a claim describes what the software does today, on your screen, with your data, it's probably real.

That's the whole tool. "Our agents will handle your entire customer service operation" is the first kind. "Paste in this 40-page contract and it produces a clause-by-clause summary in about a minute" is the second kind. You could check the second one yourself before lunch.

Everything else in this class is practice at using those two lines under pressure, in a meeting, with a confident person across the table.

## Why AI marketing is unusually thick

There are three reasons, all commercial and none mysterious.

- **They've spent a fortune.** The vendors are in a land grab and have spent billions on datacentres. The pitch has to justify the spend.
- **There's a real story to stretch.** The technology genuinely improved fast between 2022 and 2024, which handed marketers something true to inflate.
- **Quality is uneven.** These tools are excellent at some tasks and poor at others. A demo can show off the easy 20 percent of the work and let you assume the hard 80 percent goes the same way.

Knowing why the sizzle exists makes it easier to hear past it, without becoming so cynical that you miss the genuinely useful product underneath.

## Demos are theatre. Pilots are evidence.

A vendor demo is an open home. The furniture is staged, the bread is baking, and nobody opens the cupboard under the sink. The prompts are chosen in advance, the tasks are forgiving, and the retries happen off screen.

You wouldn't buy a house without a building inspection. The buyer's version for software is a **pilot test**. You bring the data and the task, using your own material, in a session you control, and you agree in writing beforehand what counts as success.

If a vendor refuses a pilot on your data, treat the refusal as the answer. It's the most informative sentence a salesperson can say.

## Five case studies, and what each one teaches

**Deloitte and the refund (October 2025).** Deloitte Australia delivered a report to the federal Department of Home Affairs, reportedly worth about $440,000. Reviewers found it contained AI-generated errors, including invented references and made-up quotations attributed to real academics. Deloitte admitted AI tools had been used contrary to its own contract terms, and refunded part of the payment.

The lesson isn't "consultants are careless". It's that a global firm with every resource still shipped confident nonsense, the bluffing student from Class 1. A person who checked the citations caught it. Checking is the product you're actually paying for.

**Klarna's 700 agents (claimed 2024, walked back 2025).** The Swedish fintech announced its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer-service agents. By May 2025, its chief executive was publicly admitting the push had gone too far on quality, and human staff were being hired back.

The lesson: "handles the volume of 700 agents" is a marketing figure with no published method. Cost cutting advertised as AI transformation often turns out to be plain cost cutting. Analysts have a name for dressing ordinary cuts in AI language: **AI washing**. Think of the washing powder that is "new and improved" every year with the same ingredients.

**Air Canada's chatbot (tribunal decision, February 2024).** A customer asked the airline's website chatbot about bereavement fares and got wrong information. The airline then argued the chatbot was a separate legal entity, responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected that argument and ordered the airline to pay.

The lesson belongs on your office wall: whatever your chatbot tells a customer, your business owns it. A chatbot is a staff member you can't fire for what it said, because legally it was you talking.

**The regulators have a word for this (March 2024, United States).** The Securities and Exchange Commission fined two investment advisers, Delphia and Global Predictions, US$225,000 and US$175,000 for claiming AI capabilities they didn't have. Australian regulators have signalled the same scrutiny under existing consumer law.

The lesson: exaggerated AI claims are becoming something regulators punish. You can fairly ask a vendor what would happen to their claims if a regulator tested them, and watch the face.

**McDonald's drive-through (trial ended June 2024).** The chain ended a trial of automated order-taking after viral videos showed the system adding bacon to ice cream and hundreds of dollars of nuggets to ordinary orders.

The lesson: when public-facing AI fails, someone films it, and the damage lands on your brand, not the vendor's.

## The sizzle vocabulary, translated

Like "cosy" and "character-filled", these words describe the seller's hopes more than the product.

- **Agentic:** the software tries to do several steps in a row on its own, instead of answering one question. How reliable that is varies wildly by task, so ask to see your task.
- **PhD-level or expert-level:** a good score on one narrow standard test, called a benchmark. It is a test result, not a job qualification. A learner driver can ace the written test.
- **10x productivity:** a multiple with no starting point. Ask: ten times what, measured how, by whom?
- **Enterprise-grade:** a pricing tier, not a quality guarantee. It describes the size of the contract, not the quality of the work.
- **Powered by AI:** sometimes means one summary button bolted onto an ordinary product. Ask which feature, exactly.
- **Seamlessly integrates:** ask which system, which version, and for a customer you can ring who uses that connection.

## Listen to the grammar

Brochures speak in the future tense. "Will", "soon", "on our roadmap" and "imagine a world where" are the sound of a listing describing the house after the renovation you'd be paying for. "Roadmap" is vendor language for features that don't exist yet.

Ask the vendor to repeat every "will" sentence as a present-tense sentence about today's product. Whatever survives is the product you can buy. The rest is a hope with a price tag.

## Take these ten questions to your next demo

1. Show me my data in your tool, not your sample data.
2. What exactly happens to data I upload, and where is it stored?
3. Do you train on my data, and which pricing tier changes that answer?
4. How does this tool typically go wrong, and who catches it when it does?
5. Which connection to my other systems works today, and which is on the roadmap?
6. Name two customers my size, in my industry, that I can call.
7. What does your contract say about who is responsible for wrong output?
8. What is the total first-year cost, including seats, minimums and setup work?
9. What happens to my data and my workflows if I cancel?
10. What did you claim last year that turned out to be wrong?

Question ten is the character test. The answers are reliably revealing, and a vendor who answers it well has earned the rest of the meeting.

## Point the test inward

The same habit works inside your own business. When a staff member says AI will save ten hours a week, ask which task, measured how, checked when. When a board paper says "we are adopting AI", replace that sentence with one claim about what the software does today, and see whether it still stands. Class 6 turns this habit into paperwork your team will actually use.

## Myth, meet reality

**"If a big, reputable company says it, the capability must be real."** Reputation and accuracy are different products.

Deloitte is one of the most established professional-services brands on earth, and still refunded part of a government report over invented citations. Klarna's chief executive personally announced the 700-agent figure and personally walked it back. Air Canada's lawyers argued, on the record, that a chatbot was a separate legal entity, and lost.

None of these organisations is stupid. Each repeated confident AI output, or an inflated AI story, because it suited a narrative, a share price or a sales cycle. A claim earns trust from the evidence behind it, never from the size of the logo beside it.

## Your exercise: roast one brochure

1. Find one piece of real AI marketing: a vendor email, a product page, a flyer or a sponsored post.
2. Underline every sentence that describes something you could check today. Circle every sentence that asks you to imagine the future.
3. Rewrite your three most circled sentences as honest present-tense claims.
4. Write down the three hardest questions from the ten above that this material carefully avoids answering.
5. Decide, in one sentence: would this vendor survive a pilot test on your data?

Notice that your honest rewrites are usually still sellable. Real capability doesn't need the sizzle, which is exactly why the sizzle is a warning sign.

## Explain it back

A colleague forwards you a vendor email that says "our AI agents will transform your business". In three sentences, with no technical words, explain to them how to tell whether it's worth a meeting. Then compare your answer with "The short version" and "The field test, in two lines".

If you couldn't say what a pilot test is in one breath, reread "Demos are theatre". If you weren't sure why big names get it wrong, reread the case studies.

## Words used in this class

- **AI washing:** describing a product, process or layoff as AI-driven when the AI part is minimal, unproven or absent. Regulators now treat it as an offence category.
- **Benchmark:** a standard test used to compare models. Useful for vendors, narrow for buyers.
- **Pilot test:** a short, agreed, measurable trial on your own data and tasks before you buy. The building inspection.
- **Agentic AI:** software that attempts several steps on its own instead of answering one question. Treat every agentic claim as unproven until you've seen it work on your own workflow.
- **Roadmap:** vendor language for features that don't exist yet. Never pay for a roadmap.
- **Failure mode:** the typical way a tool goes wrong. A mature vendor can describe theirs plainly.
- **Total cost of ownership:** seats plus minimums plus setup plus training plus the staff time spent checking output. The number that belongs in your budget.
